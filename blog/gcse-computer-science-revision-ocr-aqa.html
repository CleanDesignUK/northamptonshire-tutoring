GCSE Computer Science revision: OCR and AQA topic priorities

A practical GCSE Computer Science revision plan: programming, networks, algorithms, theory and exam practice for OCR or AQA.

Published 11 November 2026 • 6 min read

How should you revise GCSE Computer Science? Treat theory, algorithms, programming and exam questions as connected skills. Many students can memorise a definition but struggle to apply it to a situation, trace an algorithm or spot a programming error under time pressure.

1. Check your OCR or AQA specification first

OCR and AQA GCSE Computer Science specifications both cover fundamental computing ideas, but the assessment structure and exact content differ. Download the subject specification, make a topic checklist, and identify what your own school is teaching. Avoid revising from an old resource simply because it has a familiar title.

2. Make a topic map rather than one huge set of notes

Separate your revision into manageable areas: computer systems and hardware; networks and cyber security; data representation; algorithms and computational thinking; and programming concepts. Tick topics only when you can answer questions about them without relying on your notes.

3. Practise tracing algorithms on paper

Exam questions may ask you to explain or trace code. Take a short algorithm involving variables, selection or iteration and create a trace table. Follow the value of each variable line by line and check the condition at every stage. This is more useful than assuming you understand a program because you recognise the syntax.

4. Practise debugging with an explanation

If code fails, explain the difference between a syntax error, a logic error and a runtime error using examples appropriate to the language you study. Then try correcting a short faulty program. Being able to describe the reason for the fix is a good sign that your understanding is improving.

5. Learn how to answer networking and security questions

Use a table to compare common terms: LAN and WAN, wired and wireless connections, threats and prevention methods, protocols and network hardware. Then practise applying these ideas to scenarios. An answer that merely lists facts may miss the application marks.

6. Use past questions, but mark them properly

Set a short timed section. Afterwards, check the marking guidance for your board, then rewrite any incomplete explanation. Keep an error log with the date, topic, what you missed and the next question you will attempt. Don't just record a score.

How much time should you spend on theory versus coding?

That depends on your weaknesses and your qualification. Use a mixture of short theory recall, programming practice and exam-style questions, then adjust the balance when your marked work shows a pattern. A tutor can help identify whether the problem is a concept, a programming habit or the way you interpret command words.
