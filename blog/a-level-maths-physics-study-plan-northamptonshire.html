A-Level Maths and Physics: a realistic weekly study plan

How sixth-form students can plan Maths and Physics practice, review weak topics and use online tuition effectively.

Published 11 November 2026 • 6 min read

A-Level Maths and Physics both reward regular, active practice. When students in Year 12 or Year 13 are juggling lessons, homework and other subjects, the most effective plan is usually one they can repeat each week rather than an ambitious timetable that collapses after two days.

Begin with the specification

Make a list of topics from your own examination board. For A-Level Maths, separate pure mathematics from statistics and mechanics. For Physics, group topics into areas such as motion, electricity, waves and fields, where relevant to your course.

Use three labels: comfortable, improving and needs attention. Avoid calling a topic “finished” because confidence in one exercise does not guarantee success when the question is phrased differently.

An example weekly study pattern

The following is a starting point, not a rule. Adjust the time for your timetable and workload.

  • Monday: 25 minutes reviewing a recently taught topic and correcting classwork mistakes.
  • Tuesday: 35 minutes of Maths exam questions, including some older topics.
  • Wednesday: 20 minutes of Physics equations, units and short explanations.
  • Thursday: 35 minutes of Physics calculations and practical-style questions.
  • Friday: 20 minutes revisiting questions answered incorrectly earlier in the week.
  • Weekend: one longer mixed-topic session, with time to mark and reflect.

Spend time reviewing mistakes

Doing lots of questions is less useful if you never identify why an answer was wrong. For every difficult question, note whether the problem was missing knowledge, misreading the question, choosing the wrong method, algebra or arithmetic.

Then make the correction specific. If the error came from rearranging a formula, revise the algebraic operation rather than just reading the Physics notes again.

Use worked examples actively

Read the first few steps of a worked solution, then cover the rest and try to finish it. Explain why each step is valid. This helps prevent the common experience of understanding a solution while looking at it but not being able to start an equivalent question independently.

Plan around mock exams

Before a mock, focus on a manageable selection of high-priority topics and mixed-paper practice. A paper done under timed conditions is useful only if you also review it afterwards. Track recurring errors and take those to the next lesson.
